§2Why it exists — Why "Our System is 100% Available!" Is Mathematically Impossible

Why can no real system ever promise `100%` availability, and what happens when you chain 10 microservices synchronously without redundancy?

  • Hardware fails constantly at scale: In a 10,000-server fleet, disks die daily, top-of-rack switches reboot, fiber optic cables get severed by backhoes, and human engineers deploy bad configs.
  • The Series Chain Trap: If a synchronous checkout request must call 5 services in series (Gateway → Fraud → Inventory → Payment → Shipping), and each service has `99.9%` ("Three Nines") availability, your checkout endpoint's availability is: $$A_{\text{series}} = 0.999 \times 0.999 \times 0.999 \times 0.999 \times 0.999 = (0.999)^5 = \mathbf{99.5\%}$$ You promised customers Three Nines (`8.76 hours/year` max downtime), and your synchronous chain mathematically guarantees 43.8 hours/year of downtime!

3.2 The 3 Fundamental Equations of Availability & Reliability

// 1. TIME-BASED AVAILABILITY (MTBF vs MTTR):
//    MTBF = Mean Time Between Failures | MTTR = Mean Time To Recover
Availability = MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR)
//    Insight: To hit 99.99%, it is much easier to shrink MTTR (automated 10-second
//    failover instead of 45-minute human pager response) than to make MTBF infinite!

// 2. REQUEST-BASED AVAILABILITY (Yield — Google SRE Standard):
Availability (Yield) = Successful_Requests (HTTP 2xx/3xx/valid 4xx) / Total_Valid_Requests

// 3. SERIES vs PARALLEL COMPONENT COMBINATION:
A_series   = A_1 * A_2 * ... * A_n                 // Every sync dependency lowers availability!
A_parallel = 1 - ( (1 - A_1) * (1 - A_2) * ... )   // Independent replicas multiply survival!

3.3 Availability vs Reliability vs Durability (Don't Confuse Them!)

Concept Plain-English Question Concrete Example in PulseStream
Availability "Is the service responding to requests right now?" `GET /v1/feed` returns HTTP 200 within `200 ms` `99.99%` of the time.
Reliability "Does the system produce the correct, defect-free result over a continuous mission window?" A server that returns HTTP 200 in `5 ms` with somebody else's private bank balance is `100% available` but 0% reliable!
Durability "Once committed, what is the probability the data is never permanently lost over a year?" If our PostgreSQL Primary crashes for 2 minutes during failover, Availability dipped for 2 minutes, but Durability is still 100% because the data survived intact on the Synchronous Standby disk and S3 (`99.999999999%` — 11 Nines)!

3.4 SLI vs SLO vs SLA & The Error Budget (Google SRE Framework)

How do Google, AWS, and Stripe govern reliability without halting feature velocity? (Google SRE Book — Chapter 4: Service Level Objectives)

  • SLI (Service Level Indicator — The Thermometer Measurement): The exact quantitative metric measured from Box 7 telemetry, e.g., "The percentage of `POST /v1/orders` requests returning non-5xx responses in `< 250 ms` over a 5-minute window."
  • SLO (Service Level Objective — Our Internal Engineering Target): The target bound on the SLI, e.g., "`99.95%` of `POST /v1/orders` requests per 30 days must meet the SLI."
  • SLA (Service Level Agreement — The External Legal Contract with Financial Penalties): Always set looser than the internal SLO (e.g., internal `SLO = 99.95%`, external customer `SLA = 99.9%`) so internal alarms page engineers before you owe customers a 10% refund!
  • Error Budget ($\mathbf{100\% - \text{SLO}}$): If your 30-day SLO is `99.9%`, your Error Budget is **`0.1%` (`43.8 minutes` of downtime or `1,000` failed requests per million)**.
    • While Error Budget remaining $> 0\%$: Product teams can deploy fast, run chaos tests, and launch risky features.
    • When Error Budget hits $0\%$: Feature launches freeze automatically; 100% of engineering time shifts to reliability/resilience fixes until the budget replenishes.

§5Explain it to a 12-year-old (The Feynman Pass)

  • Availability vs Durability: Imagine the school library closes for 1 hour because the power went out. For that 1 hour, the library was Unavailable (you couldn't borrow a book). Did all the books inside burn to ashes? No! When the lights come back on, every single book is still safely on the shelf—that's 100% Durability!
  • SLI vs SLO vs SLA:
    • SLI is the speedometer in your mom's car reading `62 mph`.
    • SLO is your mom's personal rule: "I always stay under `65 mph` to be safe."
    • SLA is the police ticket limit at `70 mph`: you set your personal rule (`65 mph`) stricter than the police ticket (`70 mph`) so you have a `5 mph` buffer before you ever pay a fine!

§8Pitfalls & Traps

⚠️ Pitfall 1 — Assuming "Two Replicas in the Same Rack/AZ" Gives Independent Parallel Availability ($1 - p^2$): The parallel availability equation $A = 1 - (1 - A_1)(1 - A_2)$ ONLY holds if failures are statistically independent! If both servers share the same power supply, top-of-rack switch, Availability Zone, or buggy configuration push, a single event kills both simultaneously (Common-Mode Failure). Place replicas across distinct Failure Domains (separate AZs/Regions) and stagger deployments via Canaries!
⚠️ Pitfall 2 — Defining an Availability SLI Only by Server-Side HTTP 5xx Count: If your load balancer or DNS breaks and drops 100% of customer packets before they reach your Spring Boot pods, your pods log `0` HTTP 500 errors (`100% success`!) while your users experience a `100% outage`. Always combine edge/load-balancer metrics with synthetic client probes from outside the VPC!

§9Recap & Glossary

  • The Nines: `99.9%` = `8.77 hr/yr`; `99.99%` = `52.6 min/yr`; `99.999%` = `5.26 min/yr`.
  • Series vs Parallel Math: Synchronous dependencies multiply availability downward ($A_1 \times A_2$); independent parallel replicas multiply failure probability downward ($1 - F_1 \times F_2$).
  • Shrink MTTR First: Since $A = \frac{\text{MTBF}}{\text{MTBF} + \text{MTTR}}$, automating detection and failover (cutting MTTR from 30 minutes to 5 seconds) is the fastest way to gain another Nine.

Glossary

Error Budget
$1 - \text{SLO}$, representing the maximum allowable unreliability over a window (e.g., 43.8 minutes/month at 99.9%) that balances feature velocity against stability.
Graceful Degradation
Maintaining core functionality when non-critical dependencies fail (e.g., serving slightly stale cached feeds or hiding "Who's Online" badges instead of returning HTTP 500).
